## Should I call a restoration company or a plumber first?

Call the plumber first only if water is still actively flowing from a broken pipe, fitting, or fixture. Stopping the source always comes before drying anything out, and a licensed plumber is the right call for that specific repair.

Once the source is shut off, whether that's the main shutoff valve, a supply line, or a failed water heater, the job becomes a drying and damage job, not a plumbing job. That's when you call a restoration company. A plumber can fix a pipe, but most plumbing companies don't carry truck-mounted extraction units, commercial dehumidifiers, or the documentation insurers expect to see for a claim. If the leak has been going for more than a day or two, soaked drywall or subflooring may already need to come out, which is restoration work, not plumbing work.

Clean water from a supply line is Category 1 and is handled differently than Category 2 gray water, which includes washing machine or dishwasher discharge, groundwater seeping in through a foundation, or a toilet overflow with urine but no feces. A toilet overflow that contains feces, or any backup coming from beyond the trap, is Category 3 black water, the same category as sewage and storm floodwater, and it requires PPE and antimicrobial treatment under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). If you're not sure which one you're dealing with, that uncertainty alone is a reason to call a restoration company rather than guess.

## When should I call 911, the utility company, or my insurer instead?

Call 911 or shut off power at the breaker before anyone enters standing water near outlets, appliances, or a dropped ceiling with visible wiring. Electrical hazards and structural instability, like a sagging ceiling that's absorbed water, are not a restoration company's first call; they're a safety call.

If the water is coming from outside, a flooded street, rising groundwater, or an overflowing irrigation canal near the Columbia River, that's typically a flood claim, not a standard homeowners claim, and the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) handles that coverage separately. Call your insurance company once the source is stopped and mitigation has started, not before. [Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) like a burst pipe, but they typically exclude gradual leaks and sewer or sump pump backups unless you've added a water backup endorsement. If you're not sure what your policy covers, our [plain-English guide to homeowners insurance and water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through it.

## What should I do before help arrives?

Stop the water source if you can do so safely, then take photos of the damage before moving or removing anything. Those photos become the backbone of your insurance claim, and they're harder to recreate once drying equipment is in place.

Move valuables and furniture away from standing water if it's safe to do so, and avoid walking through water that's near outlets or appliances. Don't run a shop vac on anything beyond clean water, and don't try to dry out drywall or carpet pad yourself if the water has been sitting more than 24 to 48 hours; that's long enough for [mold to start growing](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), and a DIY dry-out often misses moisture trapped behind walls.
